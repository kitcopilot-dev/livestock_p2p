# Lending compliance for facilitated bank credit

Research ticket: "Research lending compliance for facilitated bank credit" (map: Bank-financed checkout). Findings capture; primary sources cited. Not legal advice — counsel sign-off is the compliance-sign-off task.

## Headline: the pilot's credit is likely business/agricultural-purpose credit
- 12 CFR §1026.3(a)(1): an extension of credit primarily for a business, commercial, or **agricultural** purpose is **exempt** from Regulation Z (TILA); §1026.3(a)(2) exempts credit to non-natural persons (eCFR, consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/3). Ranchers buying livestock for their operation = agricultural-purpose credit → no Reg Z APR/disclosure regime for the loan itself.
- The exemption turns on the loan's *primary purpose* (CFPB official interpretation, §1026.3 interp; ABA Banking Journal QotM, Oct 2024 — exemption applies even when the applicant is an individual if the purpose is business). The spec must capture purpose documentation/attestation at checkout and keep consumer-purpose loans out of the pilot.

## Who is the lender ("true lender")
- Our standing decision — the bank owns the credit decision end-to-end — aligns with the "true lender = bank" structure: the bank originates and funds all loans (Fintech Council, FAQs on Bank-Fintech Partnerships, 2021). OCC proposed a true-lender rule in 2020 (never finalized); CFPB scrutiny of bank-fintech partnerships continues, with a reduced small-dollar enforcement posture announced in 2025 (consumerfinancemonitor.com; Mayer Brown licensing outlook, Feb 2025).
- State "totality of circumstances" statutes treat the platform as the lender if it: indemnifies the bank for loan losses, predominantly designs/controls the loan program, or holds the predominant economic interest (ME Rev. Stat. tit. 9-A §2-702; 815 ILCS 123/15-5-15(b)(3)). Our standing decisions (origination fee + rev share, non-recourse) are compatible **only if** compensation is structured as service/origination fees, not loan participation — Nevada separately licenses any fintech holding a "material economic interest" in bank-funded loan revenue (Nev. Rev. Stat. §675.035(3)(c)).

## State licensing
- A marketplace that *arranges* loans for a bank may need state licenses to arrange/solicit in some states (Chapman and Cutler, "US Regulatory Landscape: Fintech Product Overview", Apr 2024): NV (soliciting; material economic interest), TN (endorsement company, Tenn. Code Ann. §45-5-102/103), HI (installment loan license for arranging consumer loans, Haw. Rev. Stat. §§480J), plus servicing/debt-collection licenses where the platform services (TX Fin. Code §342.051; Neb. Rev. Stat. §45-1005; Ga. Code Ann. §7-3-4). Florida notably does NOT require a broker license to arrange consumer loans (Chapman).
- A state-by-state licensing matrix is a must-have in the pilot spec, built with counsel, starting with the states where buyers/sellers concentrate.

## Federal regulators
- CFPB supervises nonbank "larger participants" and may examine nonbanks posing risk to consumers (2022 action); FTC regulates nonbanks for UDAP; state AGs enforce state consumer statutes (Chapman, Apr 2024).
- ECOA / Regulation B §1071 small-business lending data collection applies to *lenders* originating ≥100 covered small-business credit transactions per year (Federal Register, Nov 2025 rulemaking history). With the bank as originator, §1071 reporting falls on the bank — confirm in pilot engagement. The platform's role must not constitute a credit decision, which our standing decision (bank owns the decision) already preserves.

## What the pilot spec's compliance section must include
1. Purpose documentation at checkout (ag/business attestation) to preserve the Reg Z exemption; no consumer-purpose loans in the pilot.
2. State-by-state licensing matrix for arranging + servicing, built with counsel; applications where required.
3. Disclosures: bank-owned; the platform shows bank terms verbatim and never sets rates.
4. Compensation documented as service/origination fees, not loan participation (Nevada material-economic-interest trigger).
5. Data-sharing consent language for buyer financial data flowing to the bank, plus privacy/security.
6. Marketing rules for the platform (no misleading rate claims).
